Why the first pass reads like a terminal

Four things
make it feel cheap

None of them is the colour palette, which is where this usually gets blamed. They are all structural, and all four are cheap to fix.

01

Everything bleeds to the frame edge

A 1920-wide chyron and a 1920-wide ticker locked to the bottom edge is the single most dated thing on the canvas. Broadcast furniture bleeds because a 1998 character generator drew straight into the raster. Software doesn’t. Float every panel on a margin and the whole package jumps forward twenty years — before you have changed a single colour.

02

The dark is blue, and the room is warm

#101116 is a blue-black. The set is walnut, cream and forest green under warm even light. A cool grey slab over a warm room always reads as a separate, cheaper layer stapled on in post. Pull the ground toward espresso and the furniture starts to look like it belongs to the same photograph.

03

Fully opaque plates have no depth at all

Opacity was chosen to protect contrast, which is the right instinct aimed at the wrong control. A 94% warm-graphite panel over the picture is indistinguishable from opaque for legibility and completely different for depth — you get the faint suggestion of the room behind it, which is what makes glass look expensive.

04

Every corner is the same corner

Eight rectangles with 8 px radius is a wireframe, not an identity. The package needs one repeated geometric gesture with an opinion in it. Here it is a 22 px chamfer on a fixed diagonal — top-left and bottom-right, on every panel, without exception. One decision, applied ten times, is what makes a package look designed.

The constraintNone of this reopens the 480p question. What an encode destroys is fine detail — hairlines, small bloom, 1 px strokes, type under 26 px. It does not destroy gloss on a large surface. A 22 px chamfer, a 2 px edge light and a 46%-height sheen are all big enough to survive; a 1 px outline and a soft glow are not, and neither appears anywhere in this direction.

It still refuses to chase deb’s cobalt body colour — saturated blue is the first casualty of chroma subsampling — but it now has real margin instead of none.

The gesture

One chamfer,
one diagonal

22 px cut at top-left and bottom-right. Same corners on every panel in the package, at every size. It is the whole identity.

The voice

One grotesque,
two widths, one mono

The lane research already named Archivo as the best single-family route: one variable grotesque with a width axis from ExtraCondensed to Expanded, OFL, cleared for video titling. That replaces the Barlow Condensed and Inter pairing for everything except data. Width becomes the tool for fitting copy, so type size never has to move — which was the old package’s hardest rule to keep.

Why a mono at all

Because it is the cheapest possible way to say machine. A status line set in mono with wide tracking reads as a system reporting its own state — an IDE gutter, a build log, a room with engineers in it. It costs one font file and it is the single strongest cue in the package for the brief you gave.

Where mono must not go

Never the headline, never the card body, never a host name. Monospace at 44 px is a terminal, and a terminal is exactly the thing this revision is escaping. It stays in labels, clocks, symbols and status — small, tracked out, secondary.

What this breaks

This direction is not compatible with four project-locked tokens. Each one was locked to protect the encode, and each is replaced by a guardrail that protects it better.

Revised geometry

64 px margin all round. Rail at y=48, h 56. Wells at y=140, 580×660, with a 584×660 card between them on 24 px gutters. Chyron 64,848,1792×104. Ticker 64,972. Host cover transform becomes 0.863 from a 672×768 half — recompute before any encode test.

What carries over untouched

The 26 px essential floor. One moving row. ▲/▼ plus a sign plus colour, never colour alone. The speaker chip as mandatory redundancy, inboard. Exact name casing. No text baked into anything a model draws. Overflow by edit, wrap, ellipsis — never shrink.

To decideThis is a revision of the visual language, not of the architecture — every rectangle still maps one-to-one onto the same overlay schema, so nothing upstream changes. What needs your call is whether the four locks come off. If they do, the next step is rebuilding the acceptance stills in this language and running a real encode test, because the edge light is the one element I would not sign off on from a simulation.
